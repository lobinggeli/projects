# Postpartum Fitness & Recovery Framework

Use this framework to understand postpartum recovery, guide return-to-exercise safely, and support a partner through this process effectively.

## Core Concept

Postpartum recovery is a physiological process, not a timeline to "bounce back." The body spent 9 months adapting; full recovery takes 12–18 months minimum. Fitness return must follow a phase-gated approach: connective tissue, hormones, and neural pathways must restore before loading them. Rushing causes long-term dysfunction (prolapse, incontinence, diastasis). Going slow early creates a better long-term athlete.

### The Three Recovery Pillars

1. **Structural restoration**: Pelvic floor, core, connective tissue, joints
2. **Hormonal stabilization**: Estrogen, relaxin, prolactin, thyroid
3. **Systemic recovery**: Sleep, nutrition, nervous system regulation

---

## Recovery Timeline Overview

| Phase | Timeframe | Focus | Exercise Status |
|-------|-----------|-------|-----------------|
| **Acute** | Weeks 0–2 | Rest, wound healing, basic function | Walking only, breathing |
| **Early recovery** | Weeks 2–6 | Pelvic floor activation, gentle mobility | Light rehab only |
| **Foundation** | Weeks 6–12 | Core restoration, movement quality | Low-impact, no loading |
| **Progressive loading** | Weeks 12–24 | Strength return, cardio rebuild | Structured, progressive |
| **Full return** | Months 6–12+ | Sport-specific, performance | Cleared by physio |

**Key principle**: These are minimums, not targets. Vaginal delivery and C-section have different constraints (see below). Always follow the slower timeline when in doubt.

## Pelvic Floor: The Foundation

The pelvic floor is the most critical system to restore before any other fitness work. It's a hammock of muscles supporting the bladder, uterus, and bowel. Pregnancy and delivery stretch/compress these structures regardless of delivery method.

### Why It Matters First

- Supports all intra-abdominal pressure during exercise
- Dysfunction shows as leaking, prolapse, or pelvic pain
- Loading a dysfunctional pelvic floor with exercise makes it worse
- Many women have overactive (too tight) not weak pelvic floors — Kegels can harm in these cases

### Assessment Signs (partner should know these)

**Symptoms requiring pelvic floor physio before exercise:**
- Leaking urine when coughing, sneezing, jumping
- Feeling of heaviness or bulge in vaginal area
- Pain during intercourse
- Inability to control gas or stool
- Pelvic pressure or pain with activity

**Green light signs:**
- No leaking with impact
- Can cough/sneeze without symptoms
- Comfortable walking 30 min without pressure/pain

### Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation (Weeks 0–12)

**Phase 1 (Weeks 0–6): Neuromuscular reconnection**
- Diaphragmatic breathing: inhale expands belly, exhale engages pelvic floor naturally
- Gentle pelvic floor contractions (only if comfortable, not forced)
- No Valsalva maneuver (breath holding under load)
- No sit-ups, crunches, planks, or heavy lifting

**Phase 2 (Weeks 6–12): Progressive activation**
- Glute bridges with pelvic floor awareness
- Bird-dog (on hands and knees)
- Side-lying clamshells
- Heel slides
- Walking increases (aim for 30 min daily by week 10)

**Professional standard**: Every postpartum person should see a pelvic floor physiotherapist at 6–8 weeks regardless of symptoms. This is standard in France and recommended by ACOG (American College of OB-GYN). It's not just for problems — it's baseline care.

## C-Section vs. Vaginal Delivery: Key Differences

| Factor | Vaginal Delivery | C-Section |
|--------|-----------------|-----------|
| Primary healing site | Pelvic floor | Abdominal scar + fascia |
| Return to driving | ~2 weeks | 6 weeks |
| Lifting restriction | Light only to 6 weeks | Nothing heavier than baby for 6–8 weeks |
| Core activation | Start at 6 weeks | Delayed — scar tissue must release first |
| Return to running | 12+ weeks | 16+ weeks |
| Full sport clearance | 6–12 months | 9–18 months |

**C-section specifics:**
- Scar tissue mobilization (gentle massage from week 6) improves long-term core function
- Adhesions can cause internal restriction — pelvic floor physio essential
- The transverse abdominis (deepest core) is cut and must be re-educated carefully

## Diastasis Recti (Abdominal Separation)

~60–70% of women have diastasis recti (DR) at 35 weeks; ~30–40% persist at 6 months postpartum. It's a widening of the linea alba (connective tissue midline), not just muscle separation.

### Assessment (basic)

Lying on back, fingers placed vertically at belly button, do a small crunch and feel for a gap:
- <2 finger widths: normal/minimal
- 2–3 fingers: moderate DR
- 3+ fingers: significant DR

**More important than gap width: can she generate tension across the midline?** A wide gap with good tension is less problematic than a narrow gap with poor tension.

### What Worsens DR

- Sit-ups, crunches, oblique crunches
- Leg raises (straight leg)
- Planks (in early stages)
- Heavy lifting with breath-holding
- "Coning" or "doming" at the midline — stop any exercise that causes this

### What Helps DR

- Diaphragmatic breathing with pelvic floor coordination
- Dead bug progression
- Modified plank (on knees) when midline can hold tension
- Gradually progressive core loading under physio guidance

## Return to Exercise: Phase-by-Phase

### Phase 1: Weeks 0–6 — Gentle Reconnection

**Goal**: Heal, sleep, feed, reconnect to body

- Walking: start with 10 min, build to 20–30 min by week 6
- Breathing exercises (box breathing, diaphragmatic)
- Gentle pelvic tilts
- Ankle pumps, calf raises
- Body weight squats (from week 3 if comfortable)

**Avoid**: Running, jumping, HIIT, resistance training, heavy lifting, cycling

### Phase 2: Weeks 6–12 — Foundation Building

**Goal**: Restore movement quality, address DR and pelvic floor actively

- Glute bridges, single-leg glute bridges
- Banded clamshells, side-lying hip abduction
- Modified push-ups (incline or knee)
- Bodyweight squats with intent
- Swimming (if wound healed)
- Yoga / Pilates (postpartum-specific, not regular hot yoga)
- Walking up to 45 min

**Avoid**: Running, HIIT, heavy barbell work, jumping, impact

### Phase 3: Weeks 12–24 — Progressive Loading

**Goal**: Rebuild strength base, cardio capacity

- Low-impact cardio: elliptical, cycling, swimming
- Resistance training: machines then free weights, focusing on glutes, back, shoulders
- Goblet squats, Romanian deadlifts (light to moderate)
- Rows, lat pulldowns, overhead press
- Core: dead bugs, Pallof press, farmer's carry (light)

**Return to running test (at 12 weeks minimum):**
Can she do all of the following without symptoms?
1. Walk 30 min
2. Single-leg balance 10 sec each side
3. Single-leg calf raise ×20 each
4. Single-leg squat ×10 each
5. Jog on the spot 1 min
6. Forward bounds

If yes → begin run/walk intervals (1 min run / 4 min walk, 3× weekly)

### Phase 4: Months 6–12+ — Full Return

- Progressive running volume (max 10% per week)
- Heavy compound lifting with pelvic floor clearance
- HIIT when impact tolerance confirmed
- Sport-specific training
- High-intensity classes

## Hormonal Factors Affecting Training

### Relaxin

Produced throughout pregnancy and up to 12 months postpartum (longer if breastfeeding). Makes joints hypermobile and injury-prone.

- Joints feel stable but are not — risk of ankle sprains, hip instability, knee issues
- Avoid ballistic movements, deep stretching to end range
- Extra attention to joint stability in strength training form

### Estrogen

Drops dramatically postpartum, especially with breastfeeding. Low estrogen causes:
- Vaginal dryness (not relevant to fitness directly)
- Joint inflammation and pain
- Sleep disruption
- Mood changes that affect motivation
- Slower recovery from training

### Prolactin (Breastfeeding)

- Keeps estrogen suppressed
- Reduces bone density temporarily (recovers after weaning)
- High-impact loading on low bone density increases fracture risk
- Adequate calcium (1200mg/day) and vitamin D critical

### Thyroid

Postpartum thyroiditis affects ~10% of women (3–12 months postpartum):
- Initial hyperthyroid phase: heart racing, anxiety, fatigue — avoid intense training
- Hypothyroid phase: weight gain, fatigue, poor recovery — training feels extremely hard
- Often resolves spontaneously; check TSH at 6 months if fatigue is unexplained

## Nutrition for Postpartum Recovery & Fitness

### Baseline Requirements

| Nutrient | Non-BF Amount | Breastfeeding Addition |
|----------|--------------|----------------------|
| Calories | 1800–2200 kcal | +400–500 kcal |
| Protein | 1.2–1.6 g/kg BW | +20–25 g/day |
| Calcium | 1000 mg | +200 mg |
| Iron | 18 mg | Same (reduced loss) |
| Omega-3 (DHA) | 200 mg | +200 mg (for infant) |
| Folate | 400 mcg | 500 mcg |
| Vitamin D | 1500–2000 IU | Same |

### Key Principles

**Do not restrict calories in first 6 months**: Body needs surplus for healing and (if breastfeeding) milk production. Weight loss focus before 6 months harms both recovery and milk supply.

**Protein timing**: 25–40g protein per meal supports muscle protein synthesis and satiety.

**Hydration**: Breastfeeding increases needs to 3–3.5L/day. Dehydration causes fatigue, poor milk supply, and impairs recovery.

**Avoid**: Aggressive caloric restriction, elimination diets (unless medically indicated), fat-free diets (hormones require fat).

## Mental Health: The Hidden Recovery

Postpartum mental health significantly affects fitness capacity and motivation. This is physiology, not weakness.

### Postpartum Mood Spectrum

| Condition | Prevalence | Onset | Key Signs |
|-----------|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Baby blues | 50–80% | Days 3–5 | Weepiness, mood swings — resolves in 2 weeks |
| Postpartum depression (PPD) | 10–20% | Weeks to months | Persistent sadness, guilt, withdrawal, inability to bond |
| Postpartum anxiety (PPA) | 15–20% | Any time | Racing thoughts, constant worry, physical tension |
| Postpartum OCD | 2–9% | Weeks to months | Intrusive thoughts, compulsive checking |
| Postpartum PTSD | 4–6% | After traumatic birth | Flashbacks, avoidance, hypervigilance |

**PPD and PPA often co-occur and are underdiagnosed.**

### Exercise as Mental Health Tool

- Exercise is evidence-based for mild-moderate PPD (comparable to antidepressants)
- Even 20–30 min walking 3×/week significantly improves mood
- Group exercise or exercising with the baby (pram walks, postnatal classes) adds social benefit
- As partner: facilitating her exercise time (watching baby) is one of the highest-value supports you can provide

### Flags Requiring Professional Support

- Persistent low mood lasting >2 weeks
- Inability to sleep even when baby sleeps
- Intrusive thoughts of harm
- Feeling disconnected from baby
- Panic attacks or inability to leave home

## How to Support as a Partner

### What Helps Most

**Practical support:**
- Take over baby duty so she can exercise uninterrupted (even 45 min is transformative)
- Help track her hydration and nutrition without policing it
- Research and book the pelvic floor physio appointment
- Walk together — shared low-impact activity that's recovery-appropriate and builds connection

**Communication:**
- Ask what kind of support she wants (motivation vs. space vs. information) — don't assume
- Avoid body comments entirely — even positive ones create pressure
- Frame exercise as recovery and energy, not weight/appearance
- Celebrate non-scale victories: energy levels, sleep quality, mood, strength milestones

**Knowledge support:**
- Understand her recovery phase so you can reality-check unrealistic expectations (hers or society's)
- Know the red flags for pelvic floor dysfunction and encourage her to report them
- Recognize that bad training days are often hormonal, not motivational failures

### What to Avoid

- "When will you be back to normal?" — recovery has its own timeline
- Comparing to other women's recovery speeds
- Suggesting she "just needs to push through" fatigue — postpartum fatigue is physiological
- Over-focusing on pre-pregnancy body or weight
- Minimizing symptoms ("that's probably normal")

## Red Flags: When to Pause and Seek Help

### Stop exercise and seek medical care if:

- Heavy bleeding (more than a normal period) after activity
- Pelvic pain during or after exercise
- Visible bulge or pressure in vaginal area
- Significant leaking of urine or stool
- Worsening back or pelvic girdle pain
- Coning/doming of the abdomen during any core exercise
- Chest pain, dizziness, extreme shortness of breath
- Fever (could indicate infection)

### Specialist referrals to consider:

| Symptom | Specialist |
|---------|-----------|
| Leaking, prolapse, pelvic pain | Pelvic floor physiotherapist |
| Persistent fatigue, weight changes | GP / check thyroid, iron, vitamin D |
| Low mood, anxiety | GP or psychologist (PPD/PPA) |
| Diastasis recti (3+ fingers) | Physiotherapist specializing in women's health |
| Hip, knee, or back pain | Physiotherapist |
| Unexplained weight changes | Dietitian + GP |

## Evidence-Based Return-to-Running Guidelines

Based on the Groom, Donnelly & Brockwell (2019) guidelines, widely adopted by physiotherapists:

**Minimum pre-requisites before running:**
1. 3 months postpartum
2. No symptoms of pelvic floor dysfunction (see above)
3. Pelvic floor physio clearance
4. Can walk briskly for 30 min without symptoms
5. Can single-leg hop without symptoms

**Progressive return:**
- Week 1: 1 min run / 4 min walk × 6 (30 min total)
- Week 2: 2 min run / 3 min walk × 6
- Week 3: 3 min run / 2 min walk × 6
- Week 4: 4 min run / 1 min walk × 6
- Week 5: Continuous 20–30 min easy run
- Progress by feel — symptoms = step back one level

## Postpartum-Appropriate Exercise Classes & Formats

| Format | When Appropriate | Notes |
|--------|-----------------|-------|
| Postnatal yoga | From week 3–4 | Must be postpartum-specific, not regular yoga |
| Postnatal Pilates | From week 6 | Core-focused, pelvic floor integrated |
| Pram/stroller walking groups | From week 2 | Excellent for social connection + recovery |
| Swimming | From week 6–8 (wound healed) | Low impact, full body |
| Spinning/cycling | From week 12 | Check seat pressure on pelvic floor |
| Group HIIT | Month 6+ only | Only when pelvic floor cleared for impact |
| Regular yoga/Pilates | Month 3+ | After pelvic floor established |
| Running | Month 3+ with clearance | Follow progressive protocol |
| Weightlifting | Month 3–4 progressively | Start light, focus on form |

## Limitations

- Individual variation is high — a symptom-free woman with good pelvic floor function may progress faster; one with significant DR or prolapse may need longer
- This framework reflects evidence as of 2024–2025; guidelines continue to evolve
- This is a support reference, not a substitute for a pelvic floor physiotherapist or GP clearance
- Mental health conditions require professional assessment and treatment — this framework only supports awareness
