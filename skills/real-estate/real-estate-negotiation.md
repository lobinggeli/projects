# Real Estate Price Negotiation (Buyer Side, Switzerland / Vaud)

Use this framework to prepare, run, and close a price negotiation when **buying** a property, especially through a seller's agent (courtier) in Romandie. It condenses the main negotiation literature into a concrete playbook and adapts it to Swiss purchase mechanics.

## Core Concept

The price you get depends far more on **preparation and structure** than on talking skill at the table. Five things drive the result:

1. **Your walk-away point and your alternative.** Know your reservation price and your BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement) before you open.
2. **The seller's real situation.** Who decides, why they sell, how fast they need the money, what else they could do.
3. **The anchor.** Your first number, how you justify it, and how much room it leaves you to concede.
4. **How you concede.** Few concessions, getting smaller each time, each traded for something.
5. **Terms beyond the price.** Timing, conditions, certainty of closing, fees and contents are all currencies you can trade.

## When to Apply

- Before making a written offer (offre d'achat) on a property
- When a counter-offer comes back from the seller or their agent
- When the seller goes silent, or the agent says "another buyer is interested"
- When you are negotiating the promesse de vente / acte de vente terms (deposit, dates, conditions)

## Part 1 — What the Literature Says (and What to Take From It)

| Source | Key idea | Real estate application |
|---|---|---|
| **Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes* (1981)** — Harvard Negotiation Project | Separate people from the problem; focus on **interests, not positions**; invent options for mutual gain; use **objective criteria**; know your **BATNA** | Ask *why* they sell and *what* matters (speed, certainty, legacy, fair split between heirs). Justify your price with comparables, condition reports and renovation costs, not "it's too expensive". |
| **Chris Voss, *Never Split the Difference* (2016)** — ex-FBI hostage negotiator | **Tactical empathy**; **labels** ("It sounds like…"); **mirroring**; **calibrated "how/what" questions**; get to "that's right"; **Ackerman** bargaining (decreasing increments, precise final number, non-monetary sweetener) | "How am I supposed to get to that number when the house needs CHF 600k of work?" lets the agent solve your problem. A precise final number (e.g. 1'743'500) signals a calculated limit. Voss's 65/85/95/100 % steps are calibrated for open-haggling contexts: **in Swiss housing, use the *shape* (shrinking steps), not the percentages.** |
| **Malhotra & Bazerman, *Negotiation Genius* (2007)** | Compute your reservation value precisely; estimate theirs; the **ZOPA** (zone of possible agreement); value creation via differences in priorities | Write down your walk-away **before** emotions start. Estimate the seller's floor (outstanding mortgage, heirs' expectations, the agent's valuation, time on market). |
| **Deepak Malhotra, *Negotiating the Impossible* (2016)** | **Framing** and **process** matter as much as substance; control the frame before discussing numbers | Frame your offer as "the price for *this* house in *this* condition", not as a discount on the asking price. |
| **G. Richard Shell, *Bargaining for Advantage* (1999)** | Leverage = who has more to lose if there's no deal; relationships and norms matter | A house that has been on the market for months, or that has several heirs, shifts leverage toward the buyer. A single eager buyer in a thin market loses leverage. |
| **Stuart Diamond, *Getting More* (2010)** | Find the other side's "pictures in their head"; trade items you value differently | Heirs may care about who lives there next, keeping some furniture, or a date that fits a sibling's plans. |
| **Herb Cohen, *You Can Negotiate Anything* (1980)** | Time, information and power; deadlines are often soft; "care, but not *that* much" | Showing you are willing to walk away is your strongest card. Don't reveal your own deadline. |
| **Robert Cialdini, *Influence* (1984)** | Reciprocity, commitment/consistency, social proof, liking, authority, **scarcity** | Expect the agent to use scarcity ("other visits planned"). Use reciprocity: every concession is traded. Authority: a bank attestation and an expert report carry weight. |
| **Kahneman & Tversky / *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011)** | Anchoring, loss aversion, endowment effect | Sellers overvalue what they own and hate "losing" against a reference price. Help them change that reference price (renovation costs, condition, comparables). |

### Empirical research specific to housing

- **Anchoring works even on experts**: Northcraft & Neale (1987) showed that real estate agents' valuations of the same house moved with the listing price they were given, while the agents denied being influenced. → The first credible number on the table shapes the result, **including the asking price itself**. Counter-anchor quickly with your own justified number.
- **First offers anchor outcomes**: Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001). Making the first offer tends to favor the party who makes it, provided they're well informed. In a purchase the seller has already anchored with the asking price, so your offer is a counter-anchor.
- **Precise numbers anchor more strongly**: Janiszewski & Uy (2008); Thomas, Simon & Kadiyali (2010) on house prices. A precise figure (1'685'000) reads as "calculated", a round figure (1'700'000) as "estimated", which invites a bigger counter-move. *Nuance*: in a first written offer to Swiss heirs, a round figure backed by an argued file is also common and acceptable. Use precision mainly for the **final** numbers.
- **Seller loss aversion**: Genesove & Mayer (2001). Sellers facing a nominal loss against what they paid set higher asking prices and wait longer. → Find out when and at what price the seller acquired the property. Inherited property has no purchase-price reference, but heirs often anchor on an *estimate* (agent's valuation, tax value, what a neighbor got).
- **Agents' incentives differ from the seller's**: Levitt & Syverson (2008). Agents selling their *own* homes waited longer and got about 3.7 % more than when selling clients' homes. → The agent earns a commission of a few percent, so an extra CHF 50k on the price is worth only ~CHF 1.5k to them. The agent's main interest is that **the deal closes**, which makes them your best ally for moving the seller toward your number.

## Part 2 — Swiss / Vaud Mechanics That Change the Game

- **Nothing is binding before the notarial deed.** A sale of real estate requires a public deed (art. 216 CO). A written offer or acceptance is a declaration of intent, not a contract. Consequences: either side can walk away until the promesse/acte; a seller can still accept a better offer after "accepting" yours (gazumping); a written acceptance has moral and practical weight, but not legal force. → **Move quickly from acceptance to a signed promesse de vente** with a deposit (acompte) held by the notary.
- **The agent usually works for the seller** and is paid by them. Everything you tell the agent may reach the seller. Use this on purpose: give the agent arguments they can repeat ("the buyers' bank won't finance above X", "the work estimate is Y").
- **Your financing is your credibility.** A bank or broker attestation makes you a certain, low-risk buyer. That is worth money to a seller, especially heirs who want a clean, quick split.
- **Bank valuation caps the price you can finance.** If the bank values the house below the agreed price, you finance the gap with extra equity. "My bank values it at X" is an objective criterion, not a bluff. Use it only if it's true.
- **Transaction costs (Vaud)**: transfer duty (droit de mutation) + notary + land register (registre foncier) + mortgage-note (cédule) costs are typically paid by the buyer. Who pays them is **negotiable** and can be a lever (see "Fees in the price" below). Any such arrangement must appear openly in the deed. **Never accept an undeclared side payment ("dessous-de-table")**: it is illegal (tax fraud, false certificate in the deed) and gives the other side power over you.
- **Inherited properties (hoirie)**: all heirs must agree (unanimity). Their typical interests are a fast, clean, easy-to-explain split, avoiding conflict between siblings, sometimes a sentimental attachment, no mortgage pressure, but costs while waiting (maintenance, taxes). Often one heir drives the decision and the others follow. The agent knows who that heir is, so ask.
- **Thin markets (small lakeside communes)**: few comparables, so each argument based on comparables must be carefully selected and documented. A few high sales can distort the averages.
- **Time on market** is visible (portal history, Homegate/ImmoScout "online since", price drops). Long time on market plus no price drop means the seller's expectations are probably above the market. That gives you leverage, but also a seller who may refuse to move.

## Part 3 — The Playbook

### Step 1 · Define your numbers (in writing, before any contact)

| Number | Definition | How to set it |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | The price you aim for | Value of the house as-is: comparables, minus renovation needed to reach comparable standard, minus a discount for risk and hassle |
| **Reservation price (walk-away)** | Above this you say no | The lowest of: (a) what the bank finances at the affordability rule and LTV, (b) what the property is worth to you vs. your BATNA, (c) total project budget (price + fees + works) |
| **Opening offer** | Your first number | Low enough to leave room for 2–3 concessions, high enough to be credible (an offer that is ridiculously low can kill the relationship with heirs or offend them) |
| **BATNA** | What you do if there's no deal | Keep renting, other listings, wait 6–12 months. **Improve it actively**: visit other properties, and let the agent know you're doing so |

Rule: **never open at your reservation price**. If you do, you have no room to concede. You must then hold firm on the price and negotiate *only* on non-price terms.

### Step 2 · Estimate the seller's position

Questions to ask the agent (calibrated, open-ended):
- "What's most important to the owners in this sale?"
- "How long has the house been on the market, and how many offers have there been?"
- "How did they arrive at the asking price?"
- "Who is involved in the decision?"
- "What would make this an easy decision for them?"
- "What timing would suit them best?"

Then check independently: listing history, price drops, tax value / last transaction (registre foncier extract), state of the property (CECB energy certificate, age of heating/kitchens/bathrooms).

### Step 3 · Build the justification file (objective criteria)

A justified offer gets moved down far less than a bare number. Include:
- 3–5 comparable sales or listings, adjusted per m² and for condition
- Quantified renovation needs (a contractor's or architect's estimate is better than your own figures)
- Energy class and mandatory or likely upgrades (heating, insulation, windows)
- Your financing attestation (certainty of closing)
- Optionally a short personal note: who you are, why this house. It works especially well with heirs who care about who lives there next.

### Step 4 · Make the offer

- In writing, with a **validity date** (7–14 days). This creates a soft deadline without being an ultimatum.
- Price, conditions (financing, possibly diagnostics or permits), proposed timing, proof of funds.
- Give the agent the arguments in a form they can **re-tell** to the seller.

### Step 5 · Handle the response

| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| **Silence** | Don't bid against yourself, and never raise without a counter-offer. Shortly before the validity date, ask a calibrated question: "What's the owners' feeling about our offer?" / "Is there anything that would help them decide?" |
| **Counter-offer** | Label first ("It sounds like they feel the price doesn't reflect the house's value"), then ask: "How did they get to that figure?" Make concessions that **shrink each time** (e.g. +40k → +20k → +7.5k), each one **traded** (date, conditions, fees, contents). |
| **"Split the difference"** | Not automatically. Splitting the difference rewards the side that anchored higher. Answer with your own reasoned step. |
| **"Another buyer offers more"** | Neither panic nor call it a bluff. "That's great for the owners. If they get a firm, financed offer above ours, they should take it. Our offer stays as it is / here's our last step." Scarcity claims are often true in hot markets, so check whether the house is still being shown. |
| **"Best and final" / bidding round** | Decide your number from your reservation price, not from emotions. Use a precise figure. Avoid escalation clauses: they reveal your ceiling. |
| **Acceptance** | Lock it in right away: request the notary's draft promesse, set the date, pay the deposit. Stop negotiating at that point (no "re-trading" except for objective new facts, e.g. a hidden defect found in a diagnostic). |

### Step 6 · Use non-price currencies

Ranked by typical value to a seller relative to their cost to you:
1. **Certainty**: financing confirmed, few conditions, a short time to signature
2. **Timing**: the date that suits them (quick or deferred), flexibility on handover
3. **Simplicity**: buying as-is (no repair requests), taking or clearing the contents, heirs not having to empty the house
4. **Fees**: who pays notary and transfer duty (see below)
5. **Deposit size**: a bigger acompte signals commitment
6. **Relationship and legacy**: who lives there next, keeping something (a tree, a name, an object)

### Lever: "Fees in the price" (net-vendeur structure)

If the seller pays transfer duty and notary fees, the headline price can rise by roughly the amount of those fees while the seller's net stays identical, e.g. "CHF X net vendeur, transfer duty and notary fees borne by the seller". It helps a seller who is attached to a headline figure close to their anchor. For you, the cash needed at closing drops (fees become part of the financed price). Conditions: (1) the bank must value the property at least at the new headline price, otherwise the gap must come from equity; (2) it must be written openly in the offer, the promesse and the deed. Check with your notary and bank before proposing it.

## Part 4 — Psychology and Conduct

- **Separate the "we love it" from the "we buy it".** Visible enthusiasm at visits weakens your position. Be warm, but measured.
- **One spokesperson.** Couples should agree on numbers and roles beforehand; a partner who says "we can go higher" in front of the agent costs real money.
- **Write down your reservation price and sign it together** before negotiations start. Revise it only for **new information**, never because of negotiation pressure.
- **Use silence.** After stating a number, stop talking.
- **Ask "how" and "what", not "why".** "Why" puts people on the defensive.
- **Be likeable.** Heirs and older sellers do choose buyers they like, sometimes even at a lower price.
- **Avoid the winner's curse.** If a seller accepts instantly, your price was probably too high. Don't make that mistake twice: always keep room in the first offer.

## Checklist Before Each Exchange

- [ ] Reservation price written and agreed by both buyers
- [ ] BATNA reviewed this week (other listings visited / identified)
- [ ] Next concession decided in advance (size + what we ask in return)
- [ ] Arguments ready for the agent to re-tell (comparables, works, financing)
- [ ] Calibrated questions prepared (3)
- [ ] Spokesperson designated
- [ ] Nothing said that reveals our ceiling or our deadline

## Limitations

- This framework summarises negotiation books and published academic studies. It does not replace advice from a notary, lawyer or independent buyer's agent.
- The empirical effects cited (anchoring, precision, agent incentives) come mostly from US or lab data. Their size in the Swiss market is not measured here. Use them for direction, not for exact numbers.
- The Voss techniques come from crisis negotiation. Some (aggressive anchors, the Ackerman percentages) are poorly suited to a cordial negotiation with heirs, so adapt the tone.
- Legal points (art. 216 CO, deed requirements) are general. Check specifics with the notary.
