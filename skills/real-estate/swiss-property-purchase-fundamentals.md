# Swiss Property Purchase Fundamentals

Use this framework to navigate the core mechanics of buying residential property in Switzerland: who is allowed to buy, how financing works, what the purchase process looks like, and what it costs beyond the sticker price.

## Core Concept

Swiss home buying is a **cash-and-collateral-driven system**, not an income-driven one. Two constraints gate every purchase, both enforced by the bank (not the state):

```
Can I buy this property?

[Property Price]
      ↓
 ┌────┴────┐
 │         │
[Equity Test]   [Affordability Test]
≥20% down,     Imputed cost ≤ 33%
≥10% "hard"    of gross income
(non-pension)  (5% notional rate,
                not actual rate)
```

Fail either test and the bank will not lend — regardless of how much cash you have for the other. Both must clear independently.

## When to Apply

- You are starting to search for a property in Switzerland (primary residence or second home)
- You need to estimate how much house you can actually afford before touring properties
- You are comparing cash vs. mortgage vs. pension-fund (2nd pillar) financing
- You are a foreign national or non-resident unsure whether you're legally allowed to buy
- You are budgeting total transaction costs, not just the purchase price
- You want to understand the closing process end-to-end (offer → notary → land registry)

## Who Can Buy: Lex Koller

The **Lex Koller** (Federal Act on the Acquisition of Real Estate by Persons Abroad) restricts *who* may buy residential property, separate from financing.

| Buyer profile | Restriction |
|---|---|
| Swiss citizen | None |
| EU/EFTA national with **B or C permit**, resident in Switzerland | None — treated identically to a Swiss buyer |
| Non-EU/EFTA national with **C permit** (settlement) | None for primary residence |
| Non-EU/EFTA national with **B permit**, resident in Switzerland | Can generally buy a primary residence; restrictions apply to second/vacation homes |
| Non-resident foreigner (any nationality, no Swiss permit) | Needs a **Lex Koller authorization**; competes for a capped national quota (~1,500/year) of holiday-home permits in designated tourist zones only |
| Any foreign buyer, commercial property | **Exempt** — offices, retail, industrial, hotels as an operating business can be bought freely |

**Practical implications**:
- If you hold a Swiss B or C permit (or are Swiss/EU-EFTA with residence), Lex Koller is not a constraint for a primary residence — proceed on financing/process alone.
- If Lex Koller applies, budget **2–6 extra months** between signing and registration for the authorization to clear. The purchase contract is signed with a suspensive condition; registration only completes once authorization is granted.
- **2026 watch item**: a Federal Council consultation opened April 2026 proposing to tighten Lex Koller further for non-European buyers and investment vehicles. If you or a co-buyer falls outside the "no restriction" rows above, check current status before relying on older guidance.

## Financing & Affordability

### The Two-Tier Mortgage

| Tranche | Covers | Repayment requirement |
|---|---|---|
| 1st mortgage | Up to 65% of property value | Interest-only; no obligation to amortize |
| 2nd mortgage | The gap between 65% and your LTV (typically up to 80%) | Must be **amortized to 65% LTV within 15 years, or by age 65**, whichever comes first |

### Equity (Down Payment) Rule

- **Minimum 20%** of purchase price as equity.
- Of that 20%, **at least 10 percentage points must be "hard" equity** — savings, securities, gifts, or **3rd pillar (3a)** funds.
- The remaining up-to-10 points can come from **2nd pillar (pension fund) early withdrawal or pledge** — but only down to a floor of 10% hard equity; pension funds cannot cover the whole deposit.
- Pension fund withdrawal reduces your eventual retirement pension and has tax consequences (withdrawal is taxed separately, at a reduced rate) — this is a real trade-off, not a free lever.

### Affordability ("Tragbarkeit") — The 1/3 Rule

Banks do not use your actual mortgage rate. They stress-test at a **notional 5% interest rate**, regardless of the real rate you'll pay:

```
Imputed annual cost = (Mortgage × 5% notional interest)
                     + (1% of property value, maintenance)
                     + (Amortization of 2nd mortgage / 15 yrs)

Imputed annual cost must be ≤ 33% of gross household income
```

Equivalently: **gross household income must be at least 3× your imputed annual housing cost.** This is the binding constraint for most buyers, not the equity requirement — a large down payment does not relax it (though it does lower the mortgage amount being stress-tested).

### Rule-of-Thumb Sizing

| Property price | Min. equity (20%) | Approx. gross household income needed |
|---|---|---|
| CHF 1,000,000 | CHF 200,000 | ~CHF 190,000–210,000/yr |
| CHF 1,500,000 | CHF 300,000 | ~CHF 285,000–310,000/yr |
| CHF 2,000,000 | CHF 400,000 | ~CHF 380,000–410,000/yr |

These are approximations — actual bank affordability calculators vary by lender (maintenance assumption 1%–1.5%, amortization schedule, treatment of variable income/bonus). Always run the specific bank's calculator before treating a number as real.

## The Purchase Process

1. **Offer / reservation** — verbal or written offer to seller/agent; sometimes a reservation contract with a small deposit (not legally binding on its own in most cantons — the notarized deed is what counts).
2. **Financing confirmation** — get a mortgage pre-approval or at least an informal affordability check from your bank before committing; sellers and agents will ask for this.
3. **Notary drafting** — in French-speaking Switzerland (incl. Vaud), **the buyer chooses and pays for the notary**, since transfer duties are a buyer cost. The notary drafts the deed of sale (*acte de vente*).
4. **Signing before the notary** — both parties sign the authenticated deed in person before the notary; this is the legally binding step (a private/verbal agreement beforehand is not).
5. **Land registry entry (Registre foncier)** — the notary files the transfer; ownership legally passes on registration, not on signing. Registration also triggers the transfer tax/duty payment.
6. **Lex Koller clearance** (if applicable) — suspensive condition resolved before final registration.

## Ownership Structures

| Structure | Use case | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Sole ownership** (propriété individuelle) | Single buyer, house or land | Full control, full liability |
| **Co-ownership** (copropriété / PPE — *propriété par étages*) | Apartments/condos; shared building | You own a fractional share + exclusive rights to your unit; shared costs governed by a co-ownership regulation (règlement de PPE) and often a co-owners' association |
| **Joint ownership by spouses** (communauté/indivision) | Couples buying together | Both names on title; consequences on death/divorce/sale differ by regime chosen — get notary advice on which fits your situation |

## Closing Costs (Buyer Side)

Budget **roughly 5% of the purchase price on top of the price itself**, covering (cantonal variation applies — see the Vaud-specific skill for exact rates):

- Transfer tax / duty (droits de mutation)
- Notary fees
- Land registry registration fee
- Mortgage note (cédule hypothécaire) setup fee, if a new one is created

This is separate from, and in addition to, your 20% equity requirement — **plan for equity + ~5% closing costs in cash**, since banks generally will not finance closing costs into the mortgage.

## Ongoing Ownership Costs

- **Imputed rental value (valeur locative)**: owner-occupiers are taxed on a notional rental value of their own home as income — **this remains in force through end-2028**, with abolition scheduled for **1 January 2029**. Until then, it's offset by deducting mortgage interest and maintenance costs, which is why the "renovate now vs. later" timing question matters (see renovation skill).
- **Property/wealth tax**: the property's official tax value is added to cantonal wealth tax; a separate municipal property tax may also apply depending on canton/commune.
- **Mortgage interest**: tax-deductible against income while valeur locative exists; deductibility rules change post-2029 reform.
- **Maintenance**: either actual documented costs or a flat-rate deduction (choose whichever is higher) — deductible against the imputed rental value.

## Common Pitfalls

**Assuming your savings account balance is enough.** The 10-point "hard equity" floor plus the 5%-ish closing costs in cash catches people who planned only around the 20% headline number.

**Ignoring the 5% stress-test rate.** A property that looks affordable at today's 1.5–2% mortgage rate can fail affordability at the bank's notional 5% rate — always calculate at 5%, not the quoted rate.

**Treating pension-fund (2nd pillar) withdrawal as free money.** It reduces retirement income and triggers its own tax event; model the retirement-side impact before using it to plug the equity gap.

**Assuming a verbal agreement or reservation deposit is binding.** In Switzerland, real estate transfer requires the notarized deed — nothing before that point is enforceable in the same way.

## Limitations

This is an educational overview, not financial, legal, or tax advice. Mortgage affordability formulas, Lex Koller eligibility, and pension-fund withdrawal rules should be confirmed with your bank, a mortgage broker, and a notary before acting — especially given the active 2026 Lex Koller reform consultation, which could change eligibility for non-Swiss/non-EU buyers.
