# Family Governance & Communication Framework

Use this framework to establish governance structures, communication protocols, and family alignment mechanisms that prevent conflicts, prepare the next generation, and ensure smooth multi-generational wealth transfer.

## Core Concept

The Family Governance Framework applies a structured approach to family wealth management that balances formal processes (charters, councils, policies) with relationship-building (communication, education, shared values). Effective family governance transforms potential conflict into alignment and prepares future generations for responsible wealth stewardship.

### The Four Governance Pillars

1. **Structure**: Formal governance bodies (family council, advisory boards) and decision-making processes
2. **Communication**: Transparent protocols for information sharing, discussion, and difficult conversations
3. **Education**: Next generation preparation for wealth responsibility, business management, and family values
4. **Conflict Resolution**: Proactive prevention and structured resolution mechanisms when disagreements arise

### Governance Architecture

```
        [Family Values & Mission]
                  ↓
        [Family Charter/Constitution]
                  ↓
        ┌─────────┴──────────┐
        │                    │
   [Family Council]    [Business Governance]
   (All members)       (Shareholders, Board)
        │                    │
        ├─ Decision processes
        ├─ Communication protocols
        ├─ Conflict resolution
        └─ Next gen education
```

## When to Apply

- Multi-generational wealth families (grandparents, parents, children/grandchildren all involved)
- Family business succession with multiple heirs (formal governance prevents operational conflicts)
- Blended families or complex structures (step-children, second marriages, unmarried partners)
- Significant wealth disparity between family branches (some wealthy, some not - creates tension)
- History of family conflicts or communication breakdowns
- International/dispersed families (geographic separation requires formal communication)
- Preparing next generation for inheritance (education and responsibility)

## Family Charter Development

### Purpose of Family Charter

**What it is**: Written document defining family mission, values, governance rules, decision-making processes, and succession principles

**What it does**:
- Establishes shared family identity and purpose beyond wealth
- Defines how family makes decisions (voting, consensus, delegated authority)
- Sets expectations for next generation (education, work requirements, lifestyle)
- Prevents conflicts by establishing clear rules before disputes arise
- Guides succession decisions (business leadership, wealth distribution)

### Core Charter Components

#### 1. Family Mission & Values

**Purpose**: Answer "Why does our family wealth exist? What do we stand for?"

**Content**:
- **Family mission statement** (1-2 sentences): "The [Family Name] exists to [purpose: support education, entrepreneurship, philanthropy, etc.] across generations while maintaining [values: integrity, innovation, family unity]."
- **Core values** (3-7 values with definitions): Integrity, Work ethic, Education, Family unity, Philanthropy, Innovation, Stewardship
- **Family narrative**: Story of how wealth was created, sacrifices made, lessons learned

**Example**:
```
Mission: The Schmidt family preserves and grows our entrepreneurial legacy while supporting each generation's education, independence, and contribution to society.

Values:
1. Entrepreneurship: We celebrate business creation and calculated risk-taking.
2. Education: We invest in continuous learning for all family members.
3. Meritocracy: Wealth access is balanced with demonstrated responsibility and capability.
4. Philanthropy: We give back to the communities that supported our success.
```

#### 2. Governance Structure

**Defines**:
- Who makes what decisions (family council vs. business board vs. individual patriarchs/matriarchs)
- How decisions are made (consensus, majority vote, supermajority)
- Meeting frequency and format
- Roles and responsibilities (chairperson, secretary, facilitator)

**Example**:
```
Family Council:
- Composition: All adult family members (18+ years)
- Chair: Rotates annually among G2 (parent generation)
- Meetings: Quarterly in-person, monthly virtual
- Quorum: 60% of members
- Decisions:
  - Succession plans: 80% supermajority required
  - Annual budget: Simple majority (>50%)
  - Family charter amendments: 100% consensus
  - Charitable giving: Simple majority
```

#### 3. Wealth Philosophy & Access

**Defines expectations for**:
- Distribution timing (when heirs receive wealth: age milestones, achievement milestones, gradual)
- Conditions for access (education completion, employment history, demonstrated responsibility)
- Trust structures vs. outright distributions
- Equal vs. equitable distribution philosophy

**Example**:
```
Wealth Access Principles:
- Age 25: Access to income from trust (not principal)
- Age 30: 1/3 of inheritance if bachelor's degree + 5 years employment
- Age 40: 2/3 of inheritance if continued responsibility demonstrated
- Age 50: Full principal access
- Exceptions: Special needs, entrepreneurship support (case-by-case family council decision)
- Philosophy: Equal opportunity, not guaranteed equal outcomes
```

#### 4. Business Succession Rules

**For family business**:
- Leadership qualification criteria (education, external experience, demonstrated capability)
- Ownership vs. management separation (all heirs own, only capable ones manage)
- Non-family management option (professional CEO vs. family leadership)
- Buyout mechanisms (non-active heirs can sell shares to active heirs at fair value)
- Dispute resolution (deadlocks, strategic disagreements)

**Example**:
```
Business Leadership:
- Requirement: MBA or equivalent + 5 years external management experience
- Selection: Family council votes, 75% supermajority required
- Term: 5 years, renewable
- Performance review: Annual by family council + independent board members
- Non-family CEO option: If no qualified family member, hire professional CEO
```

#### 5. Communication Protocols

**Establishes**:
- Transparency vs. privacy (what information is shared, what remains private)
- Information sharing mechanisms (annual family meetings, quarterly updates, financial reports)
- Difficult conversation procedures (addressing underperformance, substance abuse, divorce)

**Example**:
```
Communication Standards:
- Annual State of Family Address: Patriarch/matriarch shares financial overview, business performance, succession plans
- Quarterly financial reports: All adult members receive summary (not detailed accounts)
- One-on-one sensitive discussions: Private before group (don't ambush in meetings)
- Confidentiality: Family business information is confidential (non-disclosure to spouses until marriage 5+ years)
```

#### 6. Conflict Resolution Procedures

**Step-by-step process**:
1. Direct conversation between parties (attempt resolution)
2. Mediation by neutral family member (elder or non-involved)
3. Professional mediator (external family therapist or mediator)
4. Binding arbitration (pre-agreed arbitrator, decision is final)

**Avoids**: Litigation (expensive, destroys relationships, public)

### Charter Development Process

**Phase 1: Values Discussion** (Family Meeting 1)
- Facilitate discussion: What do we stand for? What mistakes do we want to avoid? What does wealth mean to our family?
- Document shared values and mission

**Phase 2: Draft Charter** (Working Group)
- Small group (2-3 family members + advisor) drafts charter based on Phase 1 discussion
- Circulate draft for feedback

**Phase 3: Family Review** (Family Meeting 2)
- Present draft, discuss amendments, build consensus
- Vote on adoption (typically requires supermajority or consensus)

**Phase 4: Formal Execution**
- All family members sign charter
- Not legally binding (moral/relational commitment), but can be referenced in legal documents (wills, shareholder agreements)

**Timeline**: 6-12 months

## Communication Protocols

### Annual Family Meeting

**Purpose**: Update all members on family wealth, business, succession plans; build connection

**Frequency**: Annually (ideally in-person, full weekend)

**Agenda (Example)**:

| Time | Topic | Leader | Format |
|------|-------|--------|---------|
| **Friday PM** | Welcome, family updates | Chairperson | Informal social |
| **Saturday AM** | Financial state of family | CFO/Wealth advisor | Presentation + Q&A |
| **Saturday Midday** | Business performance review | CEO/Business leader | Presentation + discussion |
| **Saturday PM** | Succession planning update | Family patriarch/matriarch | Strategic discussion |
| **Saturday Evening** | Next generation education session | External expert | Workshop |
| **Sunday AM** | Family charter review/amendments | Chairperson | Voting if needed |
| **Sunday Midday** | Philanthropy/giving discussion | Philanthropy committee | Decision-making |

**Best practices**:
- Combine business with social (family dinner, activities - build relationships)
- Allow Q&A and discussion (not just top-down presentation)
- Include next generation appropriate to age (teenagers attend selected sessions)
- Document decisions and action items

### Quarterly Check-ins

**Purpose**: Maintain communication between annual meetings

**Format**: Virtual call (1-2 hours)

**Content**:
- Brief updates (business, investments, family news)
- Urgent decisions if needed
- Q&A

### One-on-One Succession Conversations

**Purpose**: Discuss individual roles, expectations, feedback privately before group announcements

**Who**: Patriarch/matriarch with each adult child

**Frequency**: Annually or as needed

**Topics**:
- Individual's career path and interests
- Potential role in family business (if any)
- Expectations for wealth access and responsibility
- Personal concerns or questions
- Performance feedback (if involved in business)

**Critical**: Have these conversations **before** announcing succession plans publicly. Avoids surprises and hurt feelings.

## Family Council Structure

### Composition

**Who participates:**
- **Core**: All adult family members (typically 18+ or 21+)
- **Optional**: Spouses/partners (decide by charter - immediate inclusion vs. after X years marriage)
- **Advisors**: Wealth advisor, family office CFO, business CEO (attend but don't vote)

### Roles

| Role | Responsibilities | Selection | Term |
|------|-----------------|-----------|------|
| **Chairperson** | Agenda setting, meeting facilitation, tiebreaking | Elected by council or rotating | 1-2 years |
| **Secretary** | Minutes, document management, communication | Appointed or volunteered | 1-2 years |
| **Treasurer** (if no family office) | Financial reporting, budgets | Appointed | Ongoing |
| **Next Gen Liaison** | Represents younger generation's interests | Elected by next gen | 1 year |

### Decision-Making

**Voting thresholds by decision type:**

| Decision Type | Threshold | Rationale |
|--------------|-----------|-----------|
| **Routine** (annual giving budget, meeting dates) | Simple majority (>50%) | Efficiency |
| **Significant** (investment strategy changes, business major decisions) | Supermajority (66-75%) | Protect against slim majority imposing on large minority |
| **Constitutional** (charter amendments, succession changes) | Supermajority (80%) or Consensus (100%) | Major changes require broad agreement |

### Meeting Frequency

- **Quarterly**: Regular council meetings (1-2 hours, can be virtual)
- **Annual**: In-person full family meeting (weekend, includes social elements)
- **Ad hoc**: Emergency meetings as needed (major decisions, crises)

## Next Generation Preparation

### Age-Appropriate Education Path

**Ages 10-15: Values & Story**
- Share family history (how wealth was created, sacrifices, lessons)
- Introduce values (work ethic, education, responsibility)
- Basic financial literacy (saving, budgeting)

**Ages 16-21: Financial Basics**
- Advanced financial literacy (investing, compound interest, debt)
- Involvement in family philanthropy (participate in giving decisions, volunteer)
- Exposure to family business (summer jobs, internships - entry level, not executive)

**Ages 22-30: Responsibility & Capability**
- Professional career outside family business (build external credibility)
- Structured involvement in family governance (attend council meetings, vote on smaller decisions)
- Graduated wealth access (trust income, small principal distributions)
- Mentorship from elder generation

**Ages 30+: Leadership**
- Eligible for family council leadership roles
- Consideration for business executive positions (if qualified)
- Full wealth access (if demonstrated responsibility)
- Begin preparing next generation (repeat cycle)

### Education Methods

**1. Family Wealth Education Sessions**
- Annual workshops at family meetings (topics: investing, estate planning, philanthropy, business strategy)
- External experts facilitate (wealth advisors, educators)

**2. Apprenticeship in Family Business**
- Entry-level roles (not executive positions)
- Mentorship by senior leadership
- Performance-based advancement (meritocracy)

**3. External Experience Requirement**
- Work outside family business for X years (typically 3-5 years) before joining family business executive level
- Builds credibility, real-world skills, external network
- Prevents entitlement ("I earned my role, not just given because I'm family")

**4. Formal Education Support**
- Family funds education (bachelor's, master's, executive programs)
- Conditional: Must complete degree, maintain grades
- Encourages continuous learning

## Conflict Prevention Strategies

### Strategy 1: Transparency Prevents Suspicion

**Problem**: Secrets breed mistrust. "Why is sibling A getting more? What are they hiding?"

**Solution**: Open communication about decisions
- **Financial transparency**: Share financial reports (appropriate detail by age)
- **Succession transparency**: Explain why certain heirs selected for roles
- **Decision rationale**: When non-equal treatment, explain "why" (business capability, special needs, prior gifts)

**Example**: "We're transferring the business to Anna because she has 10 years experience and MBA. David will receive equal value in real estate and cash. This is equitable based on capabilities."

### Strategy 2: Equal Opportunity, Not Equal Outcomes

**Problem**: Forcing equal outcomes when capabilities differ creates resentment

**Solution**: Offer equal opportunity (education, mentorship, chance to prove themselves), then allocate based on results
- All children offered business apprenticeship
- Only capable ones selected for leadership
- Others receive equal value in different assets

**Acceptance**: If process is fair and transparent, unequal outcomes are accepted

### Strategy 3: Letter of Wishes

**Problem**: Legal documents (will) don't explain "why" - just state "what"

**Solution**: Write letter of wishes (non-binding, personal message)
- Explain rationale for distributions
- Express love for all children equally
- Clarify non-equal ≠ favoritism (based on needs, capabilities)
- Reduce perception of unfairness

**Example**:
```
"To my children,

I love you equally, but your needs and contributions differ. Anna receives the business because she has dedicated 15 years to building it. David receives equivalent value in other assets because his career path led elsewhere. Both are equally valued.

My hope is you support each other and understand these decisions were made with deep thought and fairness in mind.

With love, Dad"
```

### Strategy 4: Pre-Inheritance "Compensation" Discussions

**Problem**: Lifetime gifts create perceived inequality if not documented

**Solution**: Track and equalize
- Document all major gifts (education, home down payment, wedding, business startup funding)
- If one child received CHF 100K for education, others receive CHF 100K equivalent in inheritance (or other child receives CHF 100K less)
- Transparent: All heirs aware of who received what during life

### Strategy 5: Professional Mediation Before Crisis

**Timing**: **Before** major conflict, not after

**Approach**: Engage family mediator when tension first appears
- Annual family meetings facilitated by professional (not family member)
- Mediator helps with difficult conversations (business succession, wealth disparity)
- Trained to surface hidden conflicts early, before they escalate

**Cost**: CHF 5,000-15,000/year (facilitator for annual meeting)

**Savings**: Avoid CHF 100,000+ litigation costs later

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Charter Without Implementation
**The mistake**: Creating beautiful charter document that sits on shelf, never referenced or enforced.

**Why it's a problem**: Charter only works if actually used. If family council doesn't meet, decisions aren't made per charter process, conflict resolution procedures ignored → charter is meaningless.

**The fix**: Accountability mechanisms - regular meetings scheduled (calendar blocked), chairperson responsible for enforcement, annual charter review to ensure relevance.

### Pitfall 2: Top-Down Imposition Without Buy-In
**The mistake**: Patriarch/matriarch creates charter and announces to family "This is how it will be."

**Why it's a problem**: No ownership, no buy-in. Next generation resents being told what to do. Doesn't follow charter once elder generation passes.

**The fix**: Co-create charter through family discussions. Involve all generations (age-appropriate). Build consensus, not dictate.

### Pitfall 3: No Conflict Resolution Until Crisis
**The mistake**: Wait until major blow-up (lawsuit, business deadlock, family rift) to address conflict.

**Why it's a problem**: By then, positions are entrenched, emotions high, relationships damaged. Much harder to resolve.

**The fix**: Proactive conflict prevention - professional facilitator for annual meetings, address small tensions early, have resolution process ready **before** needed.

### Pitfall 4: Excluding Spouses Creates Division
**The mistake**: Never include spouses in family discussions, treat them as outsiders even after decades of marriage.

**Why it's a problem**: Spouses influence family members' decisions. Excluding them creates "us vs. them" dynamic. Spouse feels disrespected, influences heir negatively.

**The fix**: Clear spouse inclusion policy in charter (e.g., "Spouses included in family meetings after 3 years marriage" or "Spouses included in social events, not governance voting"). Communicate openly about boundaries.

### Pitfall 5: Forgetting to Prepare Next Generation
**The mistake**: Keep next generation in dark about wealth until inheritance. No education, no preparation, sudden wealth.

**Why it's a problem**: Unprepared heirs mismanage wealth, make poor decisions, entitlement mindset, family conflict over roles.

**The fix**: Age-appropriate education path (this framework). Include next gen in family meetings, governance, business apprenticeships. Gradual responsibility increase.

## Limitations

### What This Framework Cannot Do

**This framework is a family relationship tool, not legal or psychological advice:**
- Family dynamics are complex and unpredictable; templates cannot solve deep relationship issues
- Professional family therapists or mediators may be needed for significant conflicts
- Legal enforceability varies; consult lawyer on binding vs. non-binding charter provisions

### When Professional Help Is Mandatory

**You MUST consult professionals for:**

1. **Family therapist/mediator**: Serious conflicts, substance abuse, mental health issues, estranged family members
2. **Governance consultant**: Complex multi-generational structures, family office governance, business succession
3. **Legal counsel**: Binding shareholder agreements, business governance documents, enforcement mechanisms

### Limitations of Family Charters

- **Not legally binding** (moral commitment only, unless incorporated into shareholder agreements)
- **Requires ongoing commitment** (charter only works if family actually uses it)
- **Cannot force harmony** (structure helps, but cannot eliminate all conflicts)
- **Evolves over time** (must be reviewed and updated as family changes)
