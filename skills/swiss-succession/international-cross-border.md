# International & Cross-Border Succession Framework

Use this framework to navigate Swiss succession planning with international elements: dual nationality, foreign assets, non-resident heirs, tax treaties, and cross-border structures.

## Core Concept

International succession planning requires coordinating multiple legal systems (Swiss federal law, 26 cantonal tax systems, foreign countries' laws), managing tax treaties, and resolving conflicts between forced heirship rules and foreign structures like trusts.

### The Four International Dimensions

1. **Jurisdiction Determination**: Which country's law governs inheritance (residence, nationality, asset location)
2. **Tax Coordination**: Managing multiple-country taxation and treaty provisions
3. **Legal Conflicts**: Resolving Swiss forced heirship vs. foreign structures (trusts, community property)
4. **Practical Execution**: Cross-border asset transfer, foreign probate, multi-currency estates

## When to Apply

- Dual or multiple citizenship (Swiss + foreign nationality creates choice of law)
- Foreign asset holdings (real estate, bank accounts, businesses abroad)
- Non-resident heirs (children/spouse living in foreign countries)
- International marriages (spouses of different nationalities)
- Trusts or foreign structures (Anglo-Saxon trusts, Liechtenstein foundations)
- Planned relocation abroad (moving to/from Switzerland)
- US persons in Switzerland (FATCA, US estate tax, dual-system compliance)

## Swiss Private International Law (PIL)

### Current Rules (Pre-Reform)

**Applicable law**: Determined by deceased's **last habitual residence** (domicile at death)

**Example**: Swiss citizen living in UK for 20 years → UK succession law applies (unless testator chooses Swiss law)

### 2025/2026 Reform: Revised PIL (nPILA)

**Key change**: Swiss nationals can **choose** Swiss law even if resident abroad

**Article 90 (revised)**: Swiss nationals living abroad can elect Swiss law for their worldwide estate

**Advantage**: Preserve Swiss forced heirship rules for Swiss nationals globally

**Effective date**: Expected 2025-2026 (verify implementation)

### Choice of Law

**Who can choose:**
- Swiss nationals (can choose Swiss law even if abroad)
- Non-Swiss in Switzerland (can choose nationality law)

**How to elect**: Explicit declaration in will or inheritance contract

**Example clause**:
```
"I, [Name], Swiss national residing in [foreign country], hereby elect Swiss law (Swiss Civil Code) to govern my entire succession."
```

**Limitations**:
- Cannot violate foreign country's mandatory rules (e.g., foreign real estate may still be governed by property location law)
- Foreign courts may not recognize choice (enforcement issue)

## Foreign Asset Holdings

### Real Estate Abroad: Situs Rule

**Principle**: Real estate is governed by **location country's law**, not residence/nationality

**Example**:
- Swiss resident owns vacation home in France
- **French law** governs inheritance of French property (not Swiss law)
- French forced heirship rules may apply (children's reserved portions in France)
- **French inheritance tax** applies to French property

**Planning implications**:
- Must comply with **both** Swiss and French forced heirship
- May need separate French will (or international will valid in both)
- French notary required for property transfer

### Bank Accounts & Securities Abroad

**Rule**: Generally governed by **deceased's residence** at death (not account location)

**Example**:
- Swiss resident has US bank account
- Swiss succession law applies to account
- **But** US may tax (see tax treaties)

**Exception**: Some countries assert jurisdiction over local bank accounts

### Business Interests Abroad

**Complex**: Depends on entity type
- **Foreign company shares**: Often treated as movable property (residence law)
- **Foreign real estate within company**: May be subject to location law

**Professional advice mandatory** for foreign business holdings

## International Tax Coordination

### Double Taxation Risk

**Problem**: Two countries may both tax same inheritance
- **Example**: Swiss resident dies, heir in Germany receives CHF 1M
  - Switzerland (residence): Cantonal inheritance tax
  - Germany (heir residence): German inheritance tax
  - **Without treaty**: Both tax, 40-60% combined

**Solution**: Tax treaties provide relief

### Swiss Inheritance Tax Treaties

**Countries with treaties:**
- France, Germany, Austria, Netherlands, Sweden, Denmark, Finland, United States (estate tax), United Kingdom (limited)

**Two relief methods:**

**1. Credit Method**: Pay tax in both countries, get credit for foreign tax paid
- Example: Pay CHF 100K German tax, CHF 80K Swiss tax → Get CHF 80K credit in Germany → Net CHF 100K total tax

**2. Exemption Method**: One country exempts asset from tax
- Example: Germany exempts Swiss real estate (only Switzerland taxes)

**Treaty details vary** - must check specific treaty provisions

### Foreign Reporting Requirements

**US persons (citizens, green card holders) in Switzerland:**
- **FATCA**: Report foreign (non-US) accounts if >$50K
- **FBAR**: Report foreign accounts if combined >$10K
- **US estate tax**: Applies to worldwide assets if US person (exemption: $13.6M in 2026, but changes)
- **Form 706**: US estate tax return required

**EU residents with Swiss assets:**
- **CRS** (Common Reporting Standard): Automatic information exchange
- Swiss banks report account balances to residence countries

## Dual Nationality Scenarios

### Swiss + EU Country

**Succession law**: Can choose **either** Swiss law or EU nationality law

**EU Succession Regulation**: Allows choice of nationality law or residence law

**Example: Swiss-French dual national living in Switzerland**
- Can choose: Swiss law (residence) OR French law (nationality)
- Election in will: "I elect [Swiss/French] law to govern my succession"

**Considerations**:
- Compare forced heirship rules (which is more favorable?)
- Tax implications (inheritance tax in each country)
- Asset location (foreign real estate always governed by location)

### Swiss + US

**Succession law**: Swiss law applies if Swiss resident (US law if US resident)

**US estate tax risk**: US citizens/green card holders subject to US estate tax on worldwide assets
- **Exemption**: $13.6M (2026, adjusted annually)
- **Rate**: 40% on amounts above exemption
- **Applies even if**: Living in Switzerland for decades

**Treaty**: US-Switzerland estate tax treaty provides some relief (credit for Swiss taxes)

**Example**:
- Swiss resident, US citizen, CHF 20M estate
- US estate tax applies to worldwide CHF 20M
- Can credit Swiss cantonal taxes paid
- **Planning**: Gift during life (no US gift tax for non-US recipients), expatriate (renounce US citizenship), use marital deduction if spouse is US citizen

## Cross-Border Structures

### Trusts (Anglo-Saxon)

**What is it**: Legal arrangement where assets held by trustee for beneficiaries (common in UK, US, offshore)

**Swiss forced heirship conflict**:
- Trust may attempt to disinherit compulsory heirs
- **Swiss law**: Forced heirship applies even if assets in trust
- **Result**: Heirs can claim reduction action against trust assets if compulsory portions violated

**Example**:
- Swiss resident creates Cayman Islands trust (all assets to spouse, disinherits children)
- Children (Swiss compulsory heirs) can claim reserved portions even from trust assets

**Planning**: Trust can work **if** respects forced heirship minimums (e.g., trust receives only disposable portion, compulsory heirs receive portions outside trust)

### Liechtenstein Foundations (Stiftungen)

**What is it**: Legal entity that holds assets for designated purpose (common wealth planning tool)

**Advantages**:
- Asset protection, privacy, succession planning
- Liechtenstein law (not Swiss forced heirship)

**Swiss forced heirship conflict**:
- If Swiss resident establishes foundation: Swiss forced heirship still applies
- Foundation bylaws that violate compulsory portions may be challenged

**Use case**: Hold non-Swiss assets (less Swiss forced heirship concern), or structure to respect minimums

### Community Property Regimes (Foreign)

**Countries**: US (some states), Spain, France (marriage régimes)

**Concept**: Spouses each own 50% of all assets acquired during marriage

**Conflict with Swiss law**:
- Switzerland has different property regimes (participation in acquests, separation of property, community of property)
- Marital agreement may be required to clarify

**Example**:
- Swiss resident married in California (community property state)
- Move to Switzerland → Which property regime applies?
- **Solution**: Marital agreement establishing Swiss or California regime

- **Disadvantage**: Coordination required (ensure no conflicts, both wills reference each other)

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Assuming Swiss Law Applies to Foreign Assets
**The mistake**: Swiss resident assumes Swiss will governs French vacation home.

**Why it's a problem**: Foreign real estate is governed by **location law** (French law), not Swiss law. Swiss will may be insufficient or even invalid for French property. French forced heirship may also apply.

**The fix**: Separate will for foreign real estate (complying with local law) OR international will valid in both jurisdictions + consult local lawyer.

### Pitfall 2: Ignoring US Estate Tax (for US Persons)
**The mistake**: US citizen living in Switzerland assumes Swiss estate planning suffices.

**Why it's a problem**: US estate tax applies to worldwide assets. CHF 20M estate = $22M USD → $3.5M US estate tax (on amount above $13.6M exemption). Swiss planning doesn't address this.

**The fix**: Coordinate Swiss and US planning. US estate attorney + Swiss lawyer work together. Consider expatriation (renounce US citizenship) if permanent Swiss resident.

### Pitfall 3: Trust Violates Swiss Forced Heirship
**The mistake**: Creating offshore trust that disinherits children (violates Swiss compulsory portions).

**Why it's a problem**: Swiss heirs can sue trust for reduction action (claim compulsory portions). Trust is challenged, expensive litigation, family conflict.

**The fix**: Structure trust to **respect** Swiss forced heirship minimums. Trust receives only disposable portion, compulsory heirs receive their portions outside trust.

### Pitfall 4: No Foreign Probate Planning
**The mistake**: Owning foreign real estate without understanding local probate process.

**Why it's a problem**: Heirs discover foreign probate takes 12-24 months, requires local lawyer (expensive), unexpected taxes. Assets frozen during probate.

**The fix**: **Before death**: Research foreign probate requirements, engage local lawyer, consider restructuring (sell property or transfer to entity that avoids probate).

### Pitfall 5: Dual Nationality Without Law Election
**The mistake**: Dual nationals don't explicitly elect which nationality law governs.

**Why it's a problem**: Ambiguity about applicable law. Courts in different countries may apply different laws, creating conflicts. Heirs face uncertainty.

**The fix**: **Explicit choice of law** election in will. "I elect Swiss law to govern my succession."

## Limitations

### What This Framework Cannot Do

**International succession is extremely complex:**
- This framework provides general principles but cannot replace country-specific legal advice
- Tax treaties and foreign laws change frequently; verify current law
- Each country's forced heirship, probate, and tax rules differ significantly

### When Professional Help Is Mandatory

**You MUST consult specialists for:**

1. **Cross-border lawyer**: Any international element (dual nationality, foreign assets, non-resident heirs)
2. **Foreign lawyer in each country**: Where assets located (real estate, businesses)
3. **International tax advisor**: Treaty analysis, multi-country taxation
4. **US estate attorney**: If US person (citizen, green card holder)
5. **Trust/foundation specialist**: If considering offshore structures
