# Special Situations & Edge Cases Framework

Use this framework to navigate complex succession scenarios: blended families, disinheritance, incapacity planning, unmarried partners, charitable giving, and dispute resolution.

## Core Concept

Special situations require departures from standard succession planning. These edge cases involve legal exceptions (disinheritance grounds), non-standard family structures (step-children, unmarried partners), incapacity safeguards, and philanthropic intentions that must be carefully structured within Swiss legal constraints.

## When to Apply

- Blended families (second marriages, step-children, multiple ex-spouses)
- Desire to disinherit compulsory heir (serious offense or criminal acts)
- Incapacity concerns (aging, health issues, need for adult protection mandate)
- Unmarried partners (no automatic inheritance rights)
- Significant charitable giving (foundations, bequests)
- Anticipated inheritance disputes (family conflicts, will contests)
- Non-traditional family structures (LGBTQ+ couples, adoptions, foster relationships)

## Blended Family Succession

### Step-Children: No Automatic Rights

**Swiss law**: Step-children are **NOT** statutory heirs (no automatic inheritance rights)

**Example**: Father remarries woman with 2 children from prior marriage. Father dies.
- Father's biological children: Statutory heirs (compulsory portions)
- Step-children: No rights (unless adopted or provided for in will)

### Strategies for Including Step-Children

#### Strategy 1: Formal Adoption
**Effect**: Step-child becomes legal child (full inheritance rights, compulsory portion)

**Process**: Court petition, both biological parents' consent (if alive), child consent if age 12+

**Advantage**: Full legal equality with biological children

**Disadvantage**: Irrevocable, affects relationship with biological parent

#### Strategy 2: Disposable Portion Bequest
**Effect**: Use disposable portion (50%+) to provide for step-children

**Example**: CHF 2M estate, 2 biological children
- Biological children: CHF 500K each (compulsory portions = 25% each)
- Disposable portion (CHF 1M): Split among step-children
- **Result**: Step-children receive inheritance but no forced rights

**Limitation**: Biological children get less than if no step-children provided for (may create conflict)

#### Strategy 3: Life Insurance for Step-Children
**Effect**: Purchase life insurance with step-children as beneficiaries

**Advantage**: Creates assets outside estate (not subject to forced heirship), step-children receive funds directly

**Cost**: Annual premiums

### Protecting Second Spouse vs. Children from First Marriage

**Conflict**: Second spouse needs financial security, but first marriage children want inheritance

**Options:**

**Option 1: Usufruct (Nutzniessung)**
- Second spouse receives **usufruct** (right to use and income) from estate assets
- Children receive **bare ownership** (no use/income now, full ownership when spouse dies)
- **Example**: Family home → Second spouse lives in home for life, at her death children receive full ownership
- **Advantage**: Spouse secure (housing, income), children eventually inherit

**Option 2: Marital Agreement Expanding Spouse Rights**
- Marital agreement can grant spouse **more** than statutory share (up to disposable portion limits)
- **Example**: Spouse receives 75% of estate (instead of 50%), children receive 25% (their compulsory minimum)

**Option 3: Life Insurance Equalization**
- Life insurance death benefit to first marriage children (compensates for more going to second spouse)

## Disinheritance: Legal Grounds

### When Disinheritance Is Permitted

**Swiss law**: Can only disinherit compulsory heir for **specific serious grounds**

**Article 477 Swiss Civil Code**: Disinheritance permitted if heir:

1. **Committed serious criminal offense** against testator or close family member
   - Examples: Assault, theft, fraud, serious threats
   - Must be criminal (not just civil wrong)

2. **Seriously breached family duties** toward testator
   - Examples: Abandonment, refusal to provide care when obligated, financial exploitation
   - High threshold (not just estrangement or disagreement)

### Disinheritance Procedure

**Requirements:**

1. **Explicit declaration in will**
   - Must state "I disinherit [Name] for the following reason: [specific ground]"
   - Cannot be vague ("bad relationship") - must cite specific acts

2. **Evidence of grounds**
   - Criminal conviction (best evidence)
   - Or: Documented facts (police reports, medical records, court filings)

3. **Proportionality**
   - Offense must be serious enough to justify disinheritance
   - Courts may reject disinheritance if grounds insufficient

**Example valid clause**:
```
"I disinherit my son [Name] pursuant to Article 477 Swiss Civil Code for the following grounds: In [Year], he was convicted of theft from my business (criminal case [number]) and embezzled CHF [amount]. This constitutes a serious criminal offense against me."
```

**Invalid clause**:
```
"I disinherit my daughter because we haven't spoken in 5 years."
(Insufficient ground - mere estrangement is not enough)
```

### Burden of Proof

**If challenged**: Testator's estate (executor) must prove grounds existed

**Heir's defense**: Can contest by showing (1) No offense occurred, (2) Offense not serious enough, (3) Reconciliation occurred before death

## Incapacity Planning: Adult Protection Mandate

### Vorsorgeauftrag (Adult Protection Mandate)

**What it is**: Legal document designating representative to make decisions if testator becomes incapacitated (dementia, stroke, coma)

**Covers:**
- Financial decisions (pay bills, manage investments, sell assets)
- Healthcare decisions (medical treatment, nursing home placement)
- Legal representation (sign documents, manage litigation)

**Without mandate**: Court appoints guardian (Beistand) - may not be family member, expensive court process

### Creating Vor sorgeauftrag

**Form**: Handwritten + dated + signed (like holographic will) OR Public deed (notarized)

**Content**:
```markdown
# Adult Protection Mandate (Vorsorgeauftrag)

I, [Full Name], born [Date], residing [Address], hereby designate the following person(s) to represent me if I become incapacitated:

**Primary Representative**: [Name, relationship]
- **Authority**: Financial, healthcare, legal decisions
- **Limitations**: [Any restrictions, e.g., cannot sell primary residence without court approval]

**Alternate Representative** (if primary unable): [Name, relationship]

**Healthcare Wishes**:
- Life-sustaining treatment: [ ] Desired [ ] Not desired in case of terminal illness
- Organ donation: [ ] Yes [ ] No
- Preferred care setting: [ ] Home care [ ] Nursing facility

**Financial Authority**:
- Access to bank accounts: [ ] Yes [ ] Specified accounts only
- Authority to sell assets: [ ] Yes [ ] No [ ] Only with court approval
- Manage investments: [ ] Yes [ ] Consult advisor first

**Effective**: Upon certification by [2 physicians / Court] that I am incapacitated

**Signed**: [Date]
**Signature**: __________
```

**Registration**: File with adult protection authority (Kindes- und Erwachsenenschutzbehörde KESB) - recommended for enforceability

## Unmarried Partners: No Automatic Rights

### Legal Status

**Swiss law**: Unmarried partners (domestic partnerships, concubinage) have **no automatic inheritance rights**

**Registered partnership**: Same-sex couples can register partnership → **same rights as marriage** (spouse exemption, compulsory portion)

**Unregistered partners**: No rights (hetero or same-sex if not registered)

### Strategies for Protecting Unmarried Partner

#### Strategy 1: Disposable Portion Bequest

**Maximum**: Partner can receive up to **disposable portion** (50%+ of estate)

**Example**: CHF 1M estate, 2 children
- Children compulsory portions: CHF 250K each (total CHF 500K)
- Disposable portion: CHF 500K → can go to partner

**Limitation**: Children still receive substantial portion (partner doesn't inherit everything like spouse would)

#### Strategy 2: Life Insurance

**Beneficiary designation**: Partner named as beneficiary on life insurance policy

**Advantage**: Death benefit paid directly to partner (bypasses estate, not subject to forced heirship)

**Tax treatment**: Partner may owe inheritance tax (non-relative rate, often high 20-50% in some cantons)

#### Strategy 3: Joint Ownership During Life

**Structure**: Transfer assets to joint ownership (bank accounts, real estate) during life

**Effect**: At death, partner owns 50% already (only 50% enters estate)

**Risk**: Irrevocable transfer during life (partner owns half immediately, not just at death)

#### Strategy 4: Foundation or Trust

**Structure**: Establish foundation with partner as beneficiary

**Advantage**: Assets in foundation not part of personal estate (less forced heirship exposure)

**Limitation**: Complex, expensive, still may be challenged if violates forced heirship

#### Strategy 5: Register Partnership (Same-Sex Couples)

**If same-sex**: Register partnership → **full spouse rights** (exempt from inheritance tax, compulsory portion)

**Process**: Register at civil registry office, both partners present

**Effect**: Legal equality with married couples

## Charitable Giving

### Bequests from Disposable Portion

**Allowance**: Can leave **disposable portion** (50%+) to charity

**Example**: CHF 2M estate, spouse + 2 children
- Compulsory portions: Spouse CHF 500K, Children CHF 250K each (total CHF 1M)
- Disposable portion (CHF 1M): Can go to charity

**Tax treatment**: Charitable bequests typically **exempt** from cantonal inheritance tax (most cantons)

### Establishing Charitable Foundation

**Purpose**: Create permanent foundation for charitable purposes (education, healthcare, poverty relief, arts)

**Minimum capital**: CHF 50,000-100,000 depending on canton

**Process**:
1. Draft foundation charter (purpose, governance, beneficiaries)
2. Contribute initial capital
3. Register with cantonal foundation authority
4. Obtain tax-exempt status (if applicable)

**Advantage**: Perpetual charitable impact, family legacy, tax benefits

**Disadvantage**: Irrevocable, ongoing governance requirements

## Inheritance Disputes & Resolution

### Reduction Actions (Herabsetzungsklage)

**What it is**: Legal claim by compulsory heir to reduce excessive dispositions that violate forced heirship

**When**: Testator's will gives too much to non-compulsory heirs (violates children/spouse minimums)

**Example**: CHF 1M estate, 2 children. Will leaves 100% to charity.
- Children compulsory portions: CHF 250K each (total CHF 500K)
- Children sue: Reduction action reduces charity bequest to CHF 500K, children receive CHF 500K

**Deadline**: **1 year** from discovery of violation (typically at death)

### Mediation vs. Litigation

**Mediation**:
- Neutral third party facilitates discussion
- Goal: Voluntary settlement
- **Advantages**: Faster (3-6 months), cheaper (CHF 5,000-15,000), preserves family relationships, confidential
- **Disadvantages**: Non-binding (unless parties agree), may not resolve if extreme positions

**Litigation**:
- Court adjudicates dispute
- **Advantages**: Binding decision, enforces legal rights
- **Disadvantages**: Expensive (CHF 50,000-200,000+), slow (1-3+ years), destroys family relationships, public record

**Recommendation**: Attempt mediation first, litigation as last resort

### Will Contest Grounds

**Valid grounds to challenge will:**

1. **Formal invalidity**: Holographic will not fully handwritten, not dated, not signed
2. **Lack of capacity**: Testator had dementia/mental incapacity when signing
3. **Undue influence**: Testator coerced or manipulated into will provisions
4. **Fraud**: Will forged or testator deceived about content
5. **Violation of forced heirship**: Excessive dispositions reducing compulsory portions

**Burden of proof**: Challenger must prove grounds (difficult)

**Prevention**: Use public deed will (notarized - harder to contest on capacity grounds), multiple witnesses, medical capacity assessment if elderly/ill

## Estate Settlement Process

### Timeline

| Phase | Timeline | Activities |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| **Death** | Day 1 | Death certificate, notify family/executor |
| **Immediate** | Weeks 1-2 | Secure assets, locate will, notify creditors |
| **Inventory** | Months 1-3 | Complete estate inventory, asset valuation |
| **Debt Settlement** | Months 2-4 | Pay debts, taxes, funeral expenses |
| **Distribution** | Months 4-12 | Transfer assets to heirs per will or intestacy |
| **Contested** | Years 1-3+ | If disputes, litigation extends timeline significantly |

### Executor Duties

**Responsibilities:**
- Locate and submit will to authorities
- Inventory all assets and debts
- Manage estate assets (pay bills, invest, maintain property)
- Pay debts and taxes
- Distribute assets to heirs
- Account to heirs (financial reporting)

**Compensation**: Typically 1-3% of estate value (or as specified in will)

**Liability**: Personal liability if mismanages estate (pay debts before distributions, proper accounting)

## Quick Reference: Special Situations Checklist

**Blended Families:**
- [ ] Step-children: Consider adoption OR disposable portion bequest OR life insurance
- [ ] Second spouse protection: Usufruct, marital agreement, or life insurance to first marriage children
- [ ] Clear communication about intentions (prevent surprise conflicts)

**Disinheritance:**
- [ ] Verify legal grounds (serious criminal offense or family duty breach)
- [ ] Explicit clause in will citing specific grounds
- [ ] Document evidence (convictions, police reports, medical records)
- [ ] Proportionality (offense serious enough to justify)

**Incapacity:**
- [ ] Create adult protection mandate (Vorsorgeauftrag)
- [ ] Designate representative (financial + healthcare authority)
- [ ] Specify wishes (life-sustaining treatment, organ donation, care setting)
- [ ] Register with KESB (adult protection authority)

**Unmarried Partners:**
- [ ] Use disposable portion bequest (up to 50%+)
- [ ] Life insurance beneficiary designation
- [ ] Joint ownership during life (bank accounts, real estate)
- [ ] If same-sex: Register partnership for full spouse rights

**Charitable Giving:**
- [ ] Bequest from disposable portion (exempt from inheritance tax)
- [ ] Consider establishing foundation (CHF 50K-100K minimum)
- [ ] Balance charitable intent with forced heirship compliance

**Dispute Prevention:**
- [ ] Clear, professionally drafted will (public deed if possible)
- [ ] Communication with heirs (explain non-equal distributions)
- [ ] Letter of wishes (non-binding rationale explanation)
- [ ] Mediation clause in will (require mediation before litigation)

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Assuming Step-Children Inherit
**The mistake**: Assuming step-children automatically inherit from step-parent.

**Why it's a problem**: No automatic rights → step-children receive nothing unless specifically provided for.

**The fix**: Formal adoption (full rights) OR explicit bequest from disposable portion OR life insurance.

### Pitfall 2: Invalid Disinheritance Attempt
**The mistake**: Disinheriting child for "bad relationship" or estrangement.

**Why it's a problem**: Insufficient legal grounds → heir challenges, disinheritance clause void, heir receives compulsory portion anyway.

**The fix**: Only attempt disinheritance if **serious criminal offense** or **serious family duty breach** + document evidence + explicit clause citing grounds.

### Pitfall 3: No Incapacity Plan
**The mistake**: No adult protection mandate, becomes incapacitated.

**Why it's a problem**: Court appoints guardian (expensive, not family member, delays, loss of control).

**The fix**: Create Vorsorgeauftrag now (while capacity exists), designate trusted representative, register with KESB.

### Pitfall 4: Unmarried Partner Receives Nothing
**The mistake**: Assuming long-term unmarried partner inherits.

**Why it's a problem**: No automatic rights → partner receives nothing, may face eviction from shared home, financial hardship.

**The fix**: Use disposable portion bequest, life insurance, joint ownership, or (if same-sex) register partnership.

### Pitfall 5: Will Contest Destroys Family
**The mistake**: Surprising heirs with non-equal distribution, no explanation, no discussion.

**Why it's a problem**: Heirs contest will (undue influence, lack of capacity claims), litigation, family breakdown.

**The fix**: Communication during life, letter of wishes explaining rationale, involve heirs in planning, use mediation clause.

## Limitations

### What This Framework Cannot Do

**These situations are legally and emotionally complex:**
- This framework provides principles but cannot replace legal advice for specific circumstances
- Disinheritance, incapacity, and disputes require specialized legal counsel
- Every family situation is unique; templates are starting points, not final solutions

### When Professional Help Is Mandatory

**You MUST consult specialists for:**

1. **Inheritance lawyer**: Disinheritance clauses, blended family structuring, dispute resolution
2. **Family mediator**: Conflict resolution, difficult family conversations
3. **Medical professionals**: Incapacity certification, capacity assessments
4. **Tax advisor**: Charitable foundation establishment, unmarried partner tax implications
5. **Notary**: Adult protection mandate public deed, complex will provisions
