# Swiss Succession Fundamentals Framework

Use this framework to understand and plan for Swiss inheritance law, forced heirship rules, and legal succession structures following the 2023 reform.

## Core Concept

The Swiss Succession Fundamentals Framework applies a compliance-first approach that balances legal requirements (forced heirship), tax optimization (cantonal variations), family harmony (fair distribution), and wealth preservation (structure selection). Effective succession planning in Switzerland requires understanding both federal inheritance law and 26 different cantonal tax systems.

### The Four Planning Pillars

1. **Legal Compliance**: Forced heirship rules, reserved portions, valid instruments
2. **Tax Efficiency**: Cantonal optimization, gifting strategies, business relief
3. **Family Harmony**: Communication, governance, conflict prevention
4. **Wealth Preservation**: Asset protection, generational transfer, value continuity

## When to Apply

- Planning your estate for the first time
- Following major life events (marriage, divorce, birth, death, significant asset acquisition)
- After the 2023 inheritance law reform (updating pre-2023 wills to take advantage of increased freedom)
- When acquiring significant assets or businesses (real estate, company shares, investments)
- Before relocating to Switzerland or to a different canton (tax implications vary significantly)
- When family structure becomes complex (blended families, international heirs, unmarried partners)
- Every 3-5 years as part of regular review (life changes, law changes, asset changes)

## 2023 Reform: Key Changes

The inheritance law reform that entered into force on **January 1, 2023** significantly increased testators' freedom by reducing forced heirship constraints.

### Before vs. After 2023

| Heir Category | Pre-2023 Compulsory Portion | Post-2023 Compulsory Portion | Change |
|---------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|--------|
| **Descendants (children)** | 3/4 of statutory share | **1/2 of statutory share** | ✅ Reduced |
| **Parents** | 1/2 of statutory share | **Eliminated** | ✅ Removed entirely |
| **Spouse/Partner** | 1/2 of statutory share | 1/2 of statutory share | No change |

### Increased Disposable Portion

**Example: Married couple with children**
- **Pre-2023**: Freely disposable portion = 37.5% of estate
- **Post-2023**: Freely disposable portion = **50% of estate**

This means testators can now allocate half their estate to anyone (spouse, charities, unmarried partners, specific children, etc.) without forced heirship constraints.

### Practical Impact

- **Parents no longer protected**: If you have children but no spouse, you can exclude your parents entirely
- **More freedom for blended families**: Easier to provide for step-children or second spouse
- **Charitable giving**: Larger portions can be left to foundations or charities
- **Business succession**: More flexibility to transfer business to capable heir rather than equal division

## Statutory Heirs Hierarchy

When someone dies without a will (intestate succession) or for the non-disposable portion, Swiss law defines who inherits in a strict hierarchy.

### The Three Degrees of Kinship

```
                    [Deceased]
                        ↓
    ┌──────────────┬────┴────┬──────────────┐
    │              │         │              │
[1st Degree]   [Spouse]  [2nd Degree]  [3rd Degree]
Descendants    (Always   Parents &     Grandparents &
(Children,     inherits) siblings      their desc.
grandchildren)           & their desc. (Uncles, aunts)
```

**Inheritance Priority Rules:**

1. **First degree excludes all others** (except spouse): Children and their descendants inherit. If first degree exists, second and third degree get nothing.

2. **Second degree inherits only if no first degree**: Parents and their descendants (siblings, nieces, nephews) inherit only if deceased has no children/grandchildren.

3. **Third degree inherits only if no first or second degree**: Grandparents and their descendants (uncles, aunts, cousins) inherit only if no closer relatives exist.

4. **Spouse/registered partner always inherits alongside others** (see spousal shares below).

### Statutory Shares by Family Situation

| Family Situation | Spouse Share | Children Share | Parents Share |
|-----------------|--------------|----------------|---------------|
| **Spouse + Children** | 1/2 | 1/2 (divided equally) | 0 |
| **Spouse + Parents** (no children) | 3/4 | 0 | 1/4 |
| **Spouse alone** (no children, no parents) | 100% | 0 | 0 |
| **Children only** (no spouse) | 0 | 100% (divided equally) | 0 |
| **Parents only** (no spouse, no children) | 0 | 0 | 100% |

**Example:**
- Estate value: CHF 1,000,000
- Family: Spouse + 2 children
- **Statutory distribution**: Spouse CHF 500,000 (50%), Child 1 CHF 250,000 (25%), Child 2 CHF 250,000 (25%)

## Forced Heirship (Compulsory Portions)

Forced heirship (Pflichtteil / réserve héréditaire) limits testamentary freedom by reserving portions of the estate for close relatives.

### Post-2023 Compulsory Portions

**Who has protected rights:**
1. **Descendants**: 1/2 of their statutory share
2. **Spouse/registered partner**: 1/2 of their statutory share
3. **Parents**: **None** (eliminated in 2023 reform)

### Calculating Compulsory Portions

**Formula**: Compulsory portion = Statutory share × 1/2

**Example: Spouse + 2 children, CHF 1,000,000 estate**

| Heir | Statutory Share | Compulsory Portion (50% of statutory) | Disposable Portion |
|------|----------------|--------------------------------------|-------------------|
| **Spouse** | CHF 500,000 (50%) | CHF 250,000 (25% of estate) | Can vary |
| **Child 1** | CHF 250,000 (25%) | CHF 125,000 (12.5% of estate) | Can vary |
| **Child 2** | CHF 250,000 (25%) | CHF 125,000 (12.5% of estate) | Can vary |
| **Freely disposable** | - | - | **CHF 500,000 (50%)** |

**What this means:**
- Spouse MUST receive at least CHF 250,000
- Each child MUST receive at least CHF 125,000
- Testator can allocate CHF 500,000 freely (e.g., 100% to spouse, to charity, to business successor, etc.)

## Legal Instruments for Succession Planning

### 1. Last Will and Testament (Testament / testament)

**Three valid forms:**

#### A. Public Deed (Öffentliches Testament / testament authentique)
- **How**: Dictated to notary in presence of two witnesses, notary prepares written document
- **Language**: Any Swiss official language (German, French, Italian, Romansh)
- **Best for**: Complex estates, international elements, business succession, doubt about testator capacity
- **Advantages**: Professional drafting, hard to contest, officially registered
- **Cost**: CHF 500-2,000+ depending on canton and complexity

#### B. Holographic Will (Handschriftliches Testament / testament olographe)
- **How**: Entirely handwritten, dated, and signed by testator (no typing, no witnesses required)
- **Requirements**: MUST be 100% handwritten (not typed), must include date (day/month/year), must be signed
- **Best for**: Simple estates, quick updates, privacy (no notary involvement)
- **Advantages**: Free, private, flexible, easy to update
- **Risks**: Easy to contest if unclear, may be lost or destroyed, formal errors can invalidate

#### C. Oral Will (Nottestament / testament oral)
- **How**: Declared orally before two witnesses in emergency situations only
- **Valid only**: Extraordinary circumstances (imminent death, war, epidemic, isolation)
- **Duration**: Expires 14 days after testator regains ability to make proper will
- **Best for**: True emergencies only
- **Rarely used**: Should not be relied upon for planning

### 2. Inheritance Contract (Erbvertrag / pacte successoral)

**What it is**: Binding contractual agreement between testator and beneficiaries regarding inheritance

**Key features:**
- **Bilateral**: Requires agreement of both parties (unlike will which is unilateral)
- **Binding**: Cannot be changed unilaterally (unlike will which can be revoked anytime)
- **Formal**: Must be notarized with two witnesses (public deed form)
- **Irrevocable**: Can only be changed/revoked if all parties agree or contract includes specific conditions

**When to use:**
- **Business succession**: Ensure heir commits to continuing business (condition for inheritance)
- **Renunciation agreements**: Heir waives compulsory portion in exchange for lifetime payment
- **Spousal protection**: Spouses mutually guarantee each other's inheritance rights
- **Complex family situations**: Binding agreements to prevent future disputes

**Example**: Father agrees to transfer business to daughter, daughter contractually commits to continuing business for 10 years and providing lifetime income to father's second wife.

### 3. Marital Agreement (Ehevertrag / contrat de mariage)

**What it is**: Contract between spouses defining property regime and succession rights

**Interaction with succession:**
- **Property regime choice** (participation in acquests, separation of property, community of property) affects what enters the estate
- **Can expand spousal rights** beyond statutory share
- **Affects calculation** of available estate for children

**Common uses:**
- Protecting spouse in blended family situations
- Business owner protecting company from claims
- International couples managing different legal systems

## The Six Succession Planning Principles

### Principle 1: Compliance First, Optimization Second
- Forced heirship rules are **mandatory** and cannot be circumvented domestically
- Violating compulsory portions makes will provisions **void** for excess amounts
- Heirs can claim compulsory portions even if will attempts to exclude them
- **Start with legal compliance, then optimize within permitted bounds**

**Example of violation**: Father leaves 100% to charity, attempting to exclude two children. Children can claim their compulsory portions (each entitled to 12.5% of estate minimum). Charity receives only disposable portion (50%), rest goes to children.

### Principle 2: Cantonal Awareness is Critical
- **Federal law** governs inheritance rights (who inherits what)
- **Cantonal law** governs inheritance taxation (how much tax is paid)
- 26 cantons = 26 different tax systems with rates ranging from **0% to 50%+**
- Residence at death determines cantonal jurisdiction for tax (not citizenship)

**Impact**: CHF 1M inheritance to sibling:
- **Schwyz (tax-free canton)**: CHF 0 tax = CHF 1M net
- **Geneva**: ~CHF 400,000+ tax = CHF 600,000 net
- **Planning implication**: Residence choice or gifting strategy can save hundreds of thousands

### Principle 3: Family Communication Prevents Conflict
- **Surprises cause lawsuits**: Undisclosed succession plans trigger family disputes
- **Transparency builds acceptance**: Explaining rationale (e.g., business to capable child, compensation to others) reduces contests
- **Document your reasoning**: Write letter of wishes explaining non-equal distributions
- **Involve family early**: Discuss succession during life, not revealed at death

**Statistics**: ~30% of inheritance disputes in Switzerland involve families who never discussed succession plans while testator was alive.

### Principle 4: Documentation Discipline is Essential
- **Asset inventory**: Maintain current list of all assets, accounts, real estate, business interests
- **Legal documents centralized**: Store will, contracts, marital agreement, insurance policies in one known location
- **Notify executor/family**: Ensure someone knows where documents are kept
- **Update regularly**: Review every 3-5 years or after major life events
- **Professional copies**: Provide copies to lawyer, notary, executor

**Common failure**: Heirs discover undocumented foreign accounts, digital assets, or business interests years after death.

### Principle 5: Professional Coordination Required
Swiss succession planning requires multi-disciplinary team:

| Professional | Role | When Mandatory | When Optional |
|-------------|------|----------------|---------------|
| **Inheritance Lawyer** | Legal structure, forced heirship compliance, will drafting | Complex families, business, international | Simple will, no disputes |
| **Tax Advisor** | Cantonal tax optimization, gifting strategy, business relief | Significant assets (CHF 500K+), multiple cantons | Small estates, single canton |
| **Notary** | Public deed wills, inheritance contracts, authentication | Inheritance contracts, public deed wills | Holographic wills |
| **Business Valuator** | Company valuation, share pricing, buyout calculations | Family business succession | No business interests |
| **Family Office** | Coordination, implementation, ongoing management | Ultra-high net worth (CHF 10M+) | Most families |

### Principle 6: Regular Review and Adaptation
**Triggers for succession plan review:**

**Life events:**
- [ ] Marriage, divorce, or death of spouse
- [ ] Birth or adoption of children
- [ ] Death of designated heir or executor
- [ ] Serious illness or incapacity
- [ ] Significant asset acquisition or sale
- [ ] Relocation to different canton or country

**Legal/financial changes:**
- [ ] Tax law changes (cantonal or federal)
- [ ] Inheritance law reforms
- [ ] Business valuation changes (company growth/decline)
- [ ] Real estate market shifts
- [ ] New investment holdings

**Recommended schedule:** Review every **3-5 years** minimum, or immediately upon trigger event.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Outdated Pre-2023 Wills
**The mistake**: Keeping wills created before January 1, 2023 without reviewing for new opportunities.

**Why it's a problem**: Pre-2023 wills were constrained by smaller disposable portions (e.g., 37.5% with spouse and children). The 2023 reform increased disposable portions significantly (to 50%+), but existing wills don't automatically benefit. Your will may not utilize your full new freedom.

**The fix**: Review all wills dated before 2023 with inheritance lawyer. Update to reallocate newly available disposable portion (e.g., increase spouse protection, provide for unmarried partner, charitable giving). Simple update can significantly change outcomes.

### Pitfall 2: Ignoring Cantonal Tax Variations
**The mistake**: Focusing only on federal inheritance law without considering cantonal tax implications.

**Why it's a problem**: Federal law is uniform across Switzerland, but taxation is purely cantonal. Identical CHF 1M inheritance can result in:
- Schwyz: CHF 0 tax (tax-free for all except non-relatives)
- Geneva: CHF 400,000+ tax (50%+ for siblings/distant relatives)
- **Difference: CHF 400,000**

**The fix**: Consult tax advisor specialized in cantonal inheritance tax before finalizing succession plan. Consider residence optimization, lifetime gifting (some cantons tax gifts less than inheritances), or strategic timing of asset transfers.

### Pitfall 3: Violating Forced Heirship Rules
**The mistake**: Attempting to completely disinherit children or spouse beyond permitted limits.

**Why it's a problem**: Provisions violating compulsory portions are **void by law**. Heirs can file "reduction action" (Herabsetzungsklage) to claim their compulsory portions even if will attempts to exclude them. Results in:
- Legal disputes after death
- Unintended distribution (partial intestacy)
- Family conflict and litigation costs
- Delay in estate settlement

**The fix**: **Always** calculate compulsory portions before drafting will. Ensure each compulsory heir receives at least minimum (50% of statutory share). Use disposable portion strategically for non-equal distributions. If you want to minimize specific heir's share, give them ONLY compulsory minimum and allocate disposable portion to others.

### Pitfall 4: Holographic Will Formality Errors
**The mistake**: Creating handwritten wills that violate formal requirements (partially typed, unsigned, undated, ambiguous).

**Why it's a problem**: Holographic wills are **strictly interpreted**. Common invalidating errors:
- Typed text (even single sentence) → **Entire will void**
- No date or incomplete date ("Summer 2023") → **Void or disputable**
- Not signed or unclear signature → **Void**
- Multiple contradictory versions → Confusion about which is valid

**The fix**:
- **100% handwritten** from first word to last (including date and signature)
- **Complete date** (day, month, year: "15 March 2026")
- **Clear signature** (full name as normally signed)
- **Single valid version** (revoke old versions explicitly: "This will revokes all previous wills")
- **Clear language** (avoid ambiguity)
- **Store safely** and notify executor of location

### Pitfall 5: No Family Communication Until Death
**The mistake**: Keeping succession plans secret from family members until death.

**Why it's a problem**: Surprises trigger disputes. Common scenarios:
- Business left to one child (others feel cheated, contest will)
- Unequal distributions without explained rationale (perceived favoritism)
- Unmarried partner provided for (children surprised, allege undue influence)
- **Result**: Litigation, family breakdown, delayed estate settlement, legal costs

**The fix**:
- **Communicate intentions during life** (explain rationale for non-equal distributions)
- **Family meetings** to discuss succession philosophy
- **Letter of wishes** (non-binding document explaining reasoning) attached to will
- **Transparency** about business succession and compensation mechanisms
- **Involve family in planning** (where appropriate)

**Note**: Communication doesn't require equal treatment, just explained reasoning. "Son receives business (value CHF 800K) because he's worked there 15 years. Daughter receives real estate (value CHF 800K) for equal value." Both understand fairness.

### Pitfall 6: Ignoring International Complications
**The mistake**: Assuming Swiss law applies uniformly to all assets and heirs.

**Why it's a problem**: International elements create complex jurisdictional issues:
- **Foreign assets** (real estate, accounts) may be governed by foreign law
- **Foreign heirs** may face different tax treatment or access issues
- **Dual citizenship** may allow choice of law but creates confusion
- **Trusts/foundations** may conflict with Swiss forced heirship
- **Tax treaties** may create double taxation or relief opportunities

**The fix**: Consult specialist for **any** international element:
- Assets located abroad → foreign legal advice
- Heirs residing abroad → tax treaty analysis
- Dual citizenship → choice of law strategy
- Foreign structures (trusts) → forced heirship compatibility analysis

See **International & Cross-Border Framework** for detailed guidance.

### Pitfall 7: Business Succession Liquidity Crisis
**The mistake**: Leaving illiquid business or real estate to multiple heirs without liquidity plan.

**Why it's a problem**:
- Heirs must pay cantonal inheritance tax (can be substantial) but inherit illiquid asset
- If primary asset is family business or real estate, heirs may need to **sell or mortgage** to pay taxes
- Forced sale under pressure = poor price
- Business continuity threatened

**Example**: CHF 2M business left to 3 children. Each inherits CHF 667K share. Cantonal tax (assume 10% average) = CHF 67K per child. If children don't have personal savings, they must either:
- Extract CHF 67K from business (damages business)
- Take loans (personal debt)
- Sell their shares (potentially to outsiders)

**The fix**:
- **Life insurance** to provide liquidity for taxes and equalization payments
- **Lifetime gifting** to reduce taxable estate (utilize annual gift tax exemptions where available)
- **Buyout agreements** (one heir buys out others with insurance proceeds)
- **Staggered succession** (gradual transfer during life reduces single tax hit)
- **Business relief** (qualify for cantonal 80% valuation reduction where available)

## Limitations

### What This Framework Cannot Do

**This framework is an educational tool, not legal advice:**
- Swiss succession law is complex and varies significantly by canton, family situation, and international elements
- This framework provides general principles and structures but **cannot replace** professional legal consultation
- Every family situation is unique and may have exceptions, nuances, or special circumstances not covered here
- Legal and tax rules change; this framework reflects law as of 2023-2026 period

### When Professional Consultation Is Mandatory

**You MUST consult Swiss professionals for:**

1. **Legal advice (Inheritance Lawyer / Notary)**:
   - Drafting legally valid wills or inheritance contracts
   - Complex family situations (blended families, disinheritance, incapacity)
   - Business succession structuring
   - International elements or foreign assets
   - Resolving inheritance disputes or reduction actions

2. **Tax advice (Cantonal Tax Specialist)**:
   - Cantonal tax optimization strategies (26 different systems)
   - Lifetime gifting strategies and timing
   - Business relief qualification (80% reduction rules)
   - Cross-border tax treaty implications
   - Large estates (CHF 500K+ where tax planning has significant ROI)

3. **Valuation advice (Business Appraiser)**:
   - Family business valuation for estate purposes
   - Fair buyout pricing for business succession
   - Complex asset valuation (IP, collectibles, unlisted securities)

4. **Mediation (Family Mediator)**:
   - Family conflict resolution during succession planning
   - Facilitating difficult family discussions
   - Negotiating inheritance contracts or buyout agreements
